## What is a sensor?

<Definition term="Sensor">
  An input device that provides a <strong>usable output</strong> in response to a <strong>specified physical input</strong>. A thermocouple, for example, turns a temperature difference into an electrical voltage.
</Definition>

Every robot faces the same question: *given the sensor readings, what was the world like?* Sensors range from **simple** (a bump switch) to **complex** (a skin of thousands of touch points).

| Quantity | Typical sensors |
|---|---|
| Contact | Switches, bumpers |
| Distance | Ultrasound (sonar), radar, infrared, laser |
| Light level | Photocells, cameras |
| Sound level | Microphones |
| Strain / force | Strain gauges |
| Rotation | Encoders, potentiometers |
| Smell | Chemical sensors |
| Temperature | Thermal sensors, infrared |
| Inclination | Inclinometers, gyroscopes |
| Pressure / altitude | Pressure gauges, altimeters |

### Levels of processing

| Sensor | Raw output | Processing needed |
|---|---|---|
| Switch | Open = 0 V, closed = 5 V | Almost none: one bit |
| Digital scale | A number | Calibration |
| Microphone | A waveform | Filtering, frequency analysis |
| Camera | Millions of pixel values | Heavy: computer vision to extract meaning |

### Ohm's law and the voltage divider

Most simple sensors change a **resistance**, and the controller measures a **voltage**:

$$
V = I\,R \qquad\qquad V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

If $R_2$ is the sensor (an LDR, say) and $R_1$ a fixed resistor, a change in $R_2$ changes $V_{\text{out}}$, which the robot reads with an analog-to-digital converter.

## Mechanical switches and limit switches

A **mechanical switch** produces an **on/off** signal when a mechanical input opens or closes its contacts. For example, it can detect whether a workpiece is on a machining table: open = absent, closed = present. In industry, logic 1 is typically **24 V DC** and logic 0 is **0 V**.

A **limit switch** detects the **presence or passage of a moving part**. It is actuated by a **lever**, a **roller** or a **cam**.

<SlideFigure page={9} caption="Limit switches actuated by (a) a lever, (b) a roller and (c) a cam." />

Limit switches differ mainly in their **actuator head**: push plunger, roller plunger, roller-lever plunger, fork type, adjustable roller arm, adjustable rod or spring rod. The head transmits the external force to the changeover mechanism.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why use them">
  Limit switches are <strong>cheaper</strong> than proximity or photoelectric switches and come in a <strong>wide range of sizes</strong>. The cost is that they need physical contact and have moving parts that wear out.
</Callout>

## Proximity switches

Proximity switches detect an object **without touching it**. There are three operating principles:

| Type | Principle | Detects | Typical range |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inductive** | Change in magnetic **inductance** | **Ferrous metal** only | ≈ 2–15 mm |
| **Capacitive** | Change in **capacitance** | Metal **and non-metal** (plastic, wood, liquids, grain) | ≈ 4–60 mm |
| **Magnetic** | Change in magnetic flux | Magnets | Depends on the magnet |

In general, proximity switches detect objects at about **0.5–20 mm**. The inductive and capacitive types are the most widely used in industry.

### Inductive proximity switch

A coil is wound around a ferrous core. When a ferrous object comes near one end of the core, the effective amount of metal in the magnetic circuit changes, and so the coil's **inductance** changes. A resonant (LC oscillator) circuit detects the change. A Schmitt trigger and an amplifier then switch a transistor output, giving an **on/off** device.

<SlideFigure page={22} caption="Inside an inductive proximity sensor: LC oscillator, Schmitt trigger and output amplifier." />

### Capacitive proximity switch

The sensor face is **one plate of a capacitor**. For a **metal** target, the object is the other plate: the closer it is, the smaller the gap and the higher the capacitance. For a **non-metal** target, the plates are the sensor and earth, and the object acts as the **dielectric**, changing the capacitance as it enters the field. Either way the change triggers an electronic switch.

$$
C = \varepsilon \frac{A}{d}
$$

<SlideFigure page={19} caption="Inside a capacitive proximity sensor: electrodes, RC oscillator, Schmitt trigger and amplifier." />

### Form and wiring

Proximity switches come in **cylindrical**, **rectangular** and **slot** shapes. They can run on **AC or DC** and are wired in **two-wire** (in series with the load) or **three-wire** (separate supply) connections. The load may be a relay coil, a control circuit or a **PLC digital input**.

<Callout type="example" title="Advantages over limit switches">

- Long electrical life, because nothing moves.
- Durable under vibration, fast motion and toxic environments.
- Work on DC or AC.
- Respond well to fast-approaching objects and high switching frequencies.

</Callout>

<SlideFigure page={27} caption="Typical applications: position control, counting pulses from a rotating shaft, counting objects, detecting faulty parts and controlling fill levels." />

## Photoelectric switches

**Photoelectric switches** (photocells) are solid-state sensors that detect objects at **long range**, from under 10 cm up to about **15 m**. An emitter sends a visible or infrared light beam to a receiver, and the object either **interrupts** or **reflects** the beam.

<Grid cols={3}>
  <Card title="Through-beam" tag="longest range">
    The emitter and receiver are <strong>separate units facing each other</strong>; the object <strong>breaks the beam</strong>. This gives the longest sensing distance but needs careful alignment and wiring at both ends. Break-beam sensors are found in garage doors and lift doors.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Retro-reflective" tag="one housing">
    The emitter and receiver share <strong>one housing</strong>; a <strong>reflector</strong> (corner-cube prisms or a mirror) sends the beam back. The object breaks the returning beam.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Diffuse-reflective" tag="direct reflection">
    One housing, no reflector: the beam normally <strong>does not return</strong>. When an object enters, its surface <strong>scatters</strong> some light back to the receiver.
  </Card>
</Grid>

<SlideFigure page={30} caption="A through-beam photoelectric switch: separate emitter and receiver." />

<SlideFigure page={36} caption="A diffuse-reflective switch: the object itself reflects the light." />

### Light-on vs. dark-on

| Mode | Output is energised when… |
|---|---|
| **Light-on** | Light **reaches** the receiver |
| **Dark-on** | The beam is **interrupted**, so the receiver is "in the dark" |

For a through-beam or retro-reflective switch, "object present" means **dark**. For a diffuse switch, "object present" means **light**, because the object reflects the beam back.

<SlideFigure page={38} caption="Light-on and dark-on operation for each photoelectric type." />

### Reflective opto-sensors

A small **LED emitter** and a **photodiode or phototransistor** detector sit side by side. Uses include object presence and distance detection, **line following** (markers or tape), wall and boundary tracking, and **shaft encoding**. Their readings depend on **surface colour, texture and ambient light**, so they need **calibration**.

## Light sensors (LDR)

A **light-dependent resistor** (photocell) is a variable resistor: **brighter light gives lower resistance; darker gives higher resistance**. Shielding, position and direction of the photocell matter a lot. Put in a voltage divider, it gives an analog voltage the controller can read.

## Potentiometers

A **potentiometer** ("pot") is a resistor with a sliding wiper, like a stereo's volume knob. Fixed across a supply voltage, the wiper voltage is **proportional to the shaft angle** (rotary pot) or slider position (linear pot). This makes it a cheap absolute position sensor, and it is what sits inside a hobby **servo motor**.

$$
V_{\text{wiper}} = V_{\text{supply}} \cdot \frac{\theta}{\theta_{\max}}
$$

```cpp title="read_sensors.ino"
const int LIMIT_SWITCH = 2;   // digital input, closed = HIGH
const int LDR_PIN = A0;       // LDR in a voltage divider
const int POT_PIN = A1;       // potentiometer wiper

void setup() {
  pinMode(LIMIT_SWITCH, INPUT);
  Serial.begin(9600);
}

void loop() {
  bool partPresent = digitalRead(LIMIT_SWITCH) == HIGH;
  int light = analogRead(LDR_PIN);                    // 0..1023
  float angle = analogRead(POT_PIN) * 270.0 / 1023;   // 270° pot

  Serial.print(partPresent ? "part " : "empty ");
  Serial.print(light);
  Serial.print(" ");
  Serial.println(angle);
  delay(100);
}
```

## Application: the GuideCane

The **GuideCane** is a robotic guide for blind and visually impaired people. A wheeled base at the end of a cane carries a ring of ultrasonic sensors. When it detects an obstacle it steers itself around it, and the user feels the change of direction through the handle and follows. It is a good example of sensing, deciding and acting combined in a single assistive device.

<Takeaways>

- A sensor turns a physical input into a usable (usually electrical) output; processing ranges from one bit to computer vision.
- **Limit switches** are cheap and contact-based; **proximity switches** are contactless and durable. **Inductive** ones detect ferrous metal only (2–15 mm); **capacitive** ones detect anything (4–60 mm).
- **Photoelectric** switches work at long range in three variants: **through-beam**, **retro-reflective** and **diffuse**. Each can run in **light-on** or **dark-on** mode.
- **LDRs** and **potentiometers** change resistance; read them through a **voltage divider**.

</Takeaways>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Choose the sensor" level="easy">

Pick a sensor for each task: (a) detect plastic bottles on a conveyor 30 mm away, (b) detect steel bolts 5 mm from the sensor in an oily, vibrating machine, (c) detect boxes crossing a 6 m wide loading door.

<Solution>

(a) **Capacitive** proximity switch, because it detects non-metal objects at up to about 60 mm. (b) **Inductive** proximity switch: steel, short range, and robust to oil and vibration. (c) **Through-beam** photoelectric switch, which has the longest range.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Voltage divider with an LDR" level="medium">

An LDR ($R_2$) is in series with a fixed $R_1 = 10\,\text{k}\Omega$ across 5 V. The LDR is $1\,\text{k}\Omega$ in bright light and $100\,\text{k}\Omega$ in the dark. What output voltage do you read in each case?

<Solution>

Bright: $5 \cdot \frac{1}{10 + 1} \approx 0.45$ V. Dark: $5 \cdot \frac{100}{10 + 100} \approx 4.55$ V. The output rises as it gets darker. Swap $R_1$ and the LDR if you want the opposite behaviour.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Light-on or dark-on?" level="medium">

A retro-reflective switch should turn a conveyor **off** when a box is present. Which mode drives the "stop" output directly?

<Solution>

When a box is present it blocks the beam, so the receiver is dark. The stop output should be energised in **dark-on** mode.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
